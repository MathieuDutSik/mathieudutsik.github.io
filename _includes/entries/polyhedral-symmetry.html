A polytope, or a polyhedral cone, can be given in two ways: by its vertices (or extreme rays), or by the inequalities of its facets. Going from one description to the other is the dual description problem, a basic task of computational geometry. General methods such as reverse search, double description, pyramid decomposition, beneath and beyond, and primal dual solve it directly. The output can be huge, since the number of facets can grow like n⌊d/2⌋ for n vertices in dimension d, and the polytopes coming from lattices and from combinatorial optimization are highly degenerate, with vertices lying on many more facets than the dimension requires. Degeneracy is exactly what makes the direct methods slow, and they often run out of memory or time.

The polytopes of interest, however, often have large symmetry groups G, which permute the vertices and the facets. The natural output is then not the list of facets but the list of their orbits under G, which can be many orders of magnitude shorter. I developed, step by step, a set of methods that work directly with orbits.

The adjacency decomposition method. This method, which comes from Christof and Reinelt, rests on a simple observation: a ridge, a face of codimension 2, lies in exactly two facets. The facets therefore form a graph, in which two facets are adjacent when they share a ridge, and one explores this graph instead of computing the facets all at once:

  1. find one facet, by linear programming;
  2. a facet F is itself a polytope of one dimension less, and its own facets are the ridges contained in F; compute them, which is again a dual description problem, with the stabilizer of F in G as symmetry group;
  3. across each ridge there is exactly one other facet, obtained by a flip, a small linear algebra computation;
  4. decide for each facet obtained whether it lies in an orbit already known, and put the new orbits in a queue;
  5. repeat with the next orbit of the queue, until it is empty.

The method is correct because the graph of facets is connected: by Balinski's theorem the graph of a d-dimensional polytope is even d-connected. This also gives a way to stop early. When the vertices not yet covered by the known orbits are too few, or span too small a subspace, removing them cannot disconnect the graph, and the partial list is proved complete.

Making it work at scale. The method is simple to state, but turning it into a tool for problems of real size required several components:

  • The symmetry group. The group of linear transformations preserving the vertex set is computed as the automorphism group of a complete graph whose edges are weighted by the scalar products viT Q−1 vj, with Q = ∑ vi viT, using graph automorphism software.
  • Canonical forms. Each facet, given by its set of incident vertices, is replaced by a canonical representative of its orbit under G, so that testing whether two facets are equivalent becomes a comparison, and new facets can be stored and looked up with a hash.
  • Recursion. Step 2 calls the method again. A heuristic decides, from the number of incident vertices, the size of the stabilizer, the dimension and the depth of the recursion, whether to decompose further or to call one of the general methods above directly.
  • Fast direct methods. At the bottom of the recursion most of the time is spent in the general methods, so all five of them are implemented natively in optimized C++, templated over the number type. The arithmetic is exact but fast: a rational input is scaled to integers, and the computations run over the integers without any division, with fraction-free pivoting and gcd reduction of the facet normals. They are first run with 64-bit machine integers, with a check for overflow, and only when an overflow occurs is the computation redone with multiprecision integers, skipping the facets already produced. Incidences are stored as bitsets, and the beneath-and-beyond method maintains the adjacency of the facets incrementally instead of searching for the ridges, which is one to three orders of magnitude faster on degenerate polytopes.
  • The bank. The facets of a symmetric polytope fall into few isomorphism types, and the same subproblem comes up again and again. The results are stored by canonical form and reused, which turns much repeated work into a lookup.
  • Parallelism. In the distributed version, each orbit belongs to the process given by the hash of its canonical form, so that the processes share the exploration without a central list. The database of orbits can be saved to disk, and long computations can be restarted.

The methods are implemented in the C++ library polyhedral_common.
