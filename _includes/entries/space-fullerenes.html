A fullerene is a 3-valent polyhedron whose faces are pentagons and hexagons, like the C60 molecule. A space fullerene is a tiling of three-dimensional space by fullerenes in which every edge is shared by three tiles and every vertex by four, the way soap bubbles meet in a foam. Only four fullerenes can occur in such a tiling: the dodecahedron and the polyhedra with 14, 15 and 16 faces, which have 2, 3 and 4 hexagons.

Pieces of periodic space fullerenes; the first one is the A15 structure.

These tilings appear in several sciences under different names. In metallurgy they are the Frank–Kasper phases of complex alloys, where the tiles are the coordination polyhedra of the atoms: the A15 phase of Cr3Si, the cubic Laves phase MgCu2, the σ phase and others. In chemistry they describe the frameworks of clathrate hydrates and zeolites, where water or silicon atoms sit at the vertices and small molecules are trapped in the cells. In geometry they are candidates for the Kelvin problem, which asks how to divide space into cells of equal volume with the least surface area: the Weaire–Phelan foam, which improved on Kelvin's 1887 solution, is the A15 structure.

The known Frank–Kasper structures had been found one by one in real crystals. With Olaf Delgado-Friedrichs and Michel Deza, I designed a computer enumeration of periodic space fullerenes with a small number of tiles in a fundamental domain. The search builds the tiling step by step, using the strong local rules on edges and vertices, and recognizes identical structures with tools for the topology of periodic tilings. It found many new Frank–Kasper structures, and the second paper extends the search to larger fundamental domains. The resulting list is a catalogue of candidate structures for crystallographers.
